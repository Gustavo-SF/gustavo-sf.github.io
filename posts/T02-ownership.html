Ownership in the new AI Revolution

Most people can agree that ownership is one of the biggest enablers for one person to feel motivated and be able to work hard for the success of one project. This is highly relevant for engineering teams as most of what they build needs to be reliable, maintained, and ready to scale. If people were simply driven by objectives, people wouldn't do much more than achieving the main objective, thinking little about what the future will throw at them. While before we could ensure teams were collaborating in the details of implementation, actively strengthening the codebase, now AI writes much of our code, and that sense of ownership is under threat.

Why We Need Ownership

Even the most methodical and detailed leader won't be able to provide all the details that are needed to achieve an objective. Of course there are several different levels of detail we can reach with a plan, and people have found all different names for them: Epics, User Stories, Features, Tasks, Issues. However much we try to plan, we will always have more things to plan within that original plan, an Issue can have multiple Sub-Issues, and those can have even more. The idea is to find the perfect level of planning that lets you anticipate all the challenges before heading out into the actual implementation. The more experience one person has, the less planning should be needed, but nevertheless, it should still be done.

Experience comes from everywhere — books, past projects, side-projects — but the most valuable kind is the deep familiarity with the project you are working on right now. The longer a project runs, the more this intrinsic knowledge matters. Ownership is what makes us build and retain it, leading to better planning and better implementation.

If we don't have ownership, aside from a certain feeling of dissatisfaction of working on something that is not yours, it also makes us frustrated, out of place, and could potentially lead to higher levels of stress if it starts consuming a lot of our time. This is all empiric to me, so it could be debated for sure.

Old Strategies for Ownership

I have been paying attention in the past years to ways of structuring software development teams, and one of the things I have seen is that structure helps a lot with the ownership of the code.

So the idea is that we can have a structure that enables ownership. This structure should make everyone in the team involved in decision making. They should have enough opportunities to be critical and to leave their footprint wherever they must:

  • All their opinions are heard and taken into consideration in future development
  • They have visibility over what is being added and what comes next
  • They have enough space and time to reflect about what could be improved

Unfortunately, there are some projects that tend to segregate development, and make sure that one's part of the code is not someone else's responsibility. This is done by making team members independent and focused only on their task, which is nice for speed, not so great for the future. In my idea, this is dangerously stepping into unsustainable territory, since knowledge and ownership get scattered.

Problem with AI

While before we could structure teams to be able to achieve a certain level of ownership, now we have another challenge which is that AI writes the code for us. Developers have been detaching more and more of their connection to the written code, saying things like "AI did it!". And I am not going to challenge this idea, if AI really did it, and we are riding its wave, I should probably just accept that it is the new norm. "AI is the one writing the code now" and we are the ones guiding it. The problem I face now is: How do we motivate ourselves to do a good job at it? How do we feel the ownership of the project?

Many software developers are still writing some code by themselves, and this decision is not simply because they are hesitant about trying the "new way of doing it with AI", but also because it is what gives them more control, more fun. But now I will challenge this idea, since if we will ever be part of this work revolution we need to find a way to adopt AI as the worker, and still have enough control while having fun.

Having Ownership in the AI Revolution

If AI is the new worker, then the old software engineers are now the new managers. So I think it makes sense to wonder about how a manager is responsible for its work, even if they are not in the battlefield of developing the project. These can be top-down process changes driven by a lead, or habits that individual developers adopt on their own.

Reporting

One of the things is Reporting and Communication, AI should report often. We needn't check everything the AI does, since that is now the responsibility of whoever is creating these AI agents, but we must have enough details of the development to make sure it aligns with the objectives and that quality holds up. Reporting can be automated but the idea is that everyone is still getting enough details that make them feel like they can still have a say and have an overview of their project. For example, a lead could add automated summarization to the project, so that after every AI-assisted session the team gets a brief changelog of what changed and why. Developers, on their side, can ask the AI to explain what it did and what tradeoffs it made before moving on.

Becoming More Knowledgeable

Another one is Knowledge. Good managers are up to date with the state of the art, most of the terms and their meaning. They don't need to know the maths or exactly how something is implemented, but have enough information to question it and dig deeper when needed. For example, anyone working in Machine Learning should know what an F1-Score is (a metric that balances precision and recall), which is essential to discuss results.

Before, we would complain that we were spending a lot of time debugging or developing a lot of boring code, and that this was not allowing us to spend time researching and learning new things. That is no longer an excuse. AI helps us debug a lot faster or at least spend some time investigating issues, while we can now learn a lot faster with AI, get personalized explanations and make sure we understand everything we are working with. Leaders can help here by making sure developers have dedicated time to learn and deepen their understanding, instead of filling every freed-up hour with more tasks. Developers should use that time to ask AI to break down unfamiliar concepts, explain the patterns behind what it wrote, or walk through the alternatives it considered.

Another Level of Ownership

Another important thing that could happen is Shifting our Ownership. While before we wanted developers to own the codebase, now we might be able to make them own the entire project. Spending less time coding means they can focus on other things, such as assuming more of what a Tech Lead would be responsible for, and discussing architectural decisions. They won't need to be trapped in the details and can think more about the features and how they fit in the overall objective. Leaders can enable this by involving developers more in feature planning and decision-making, not just implementation. And developers should start treating AI the way a good lead treats their team — guiding it towards the right solution rather than just accepting whatever comes out.

This topic is delicate to me since I do care a lot about being part of a strong and motivated team. I have seen what happens when ownership fades — people detach, collaboration becomes shallow, and a team starts resembling a shared interest group where people exchange ideas but never truly build together. No one wants this, and the project will hardly go further than it would with a single person.

It is also important to note that in the middle of all this change it is perfectly reasonable that people don't like the new "job" they are getting, which is completely fair and maybe they can find something that still suits what they initially expected as a developer. On the other hand, they can also see it as a major opportunity since this new ownership brings other responsibilities — developers can have lead-type of roles earlier, if not from the very start of their careers.

So it seems even in this AI revolution we can still do something about people's sense of ownership. We must embrace change and make sure that we adapt, and here are a few ideas to start with: reporting keeps us in the loop, knowledge keeps us sharp, and shifting ownership lets us think bigger. The code may be AI's now — but the whole project is now ours.
