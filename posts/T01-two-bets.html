Two Bets

During the past 2-3 years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been slowly integrating all aspects of our lives. It started as a nice enabler to do boring tasks — auto-completing code, drafting emails, summarizing documents — making a big part of routine work mostly disappear. But we were simply looking at it in one dimension; we hadn't really thought about how it could replace other parts of our work.

I am a software engineer and I have been thinking a lot about how AI is going to reshape the industry. I know everyone has been writing about it, and that's great — I have drawn inspiration from some of those people too. I created this blog because these thoughts have been plaguing my mind. If organizing them here helps someone else too, even better.

I think there are two things worth betting on right now: blueprinting and knowledge management.

1. Blueprinting

Here I am talking about the capacity to sketch and understand the structure of what AI is building; this can then be used to understand its faults and how it can scale. Of course AI can already produce a plan, but we still have to review it, which in my opinion is a tedious process. Simply reviewing plans from AI could potentially kill creativity and could also take longer, since at the moment the knowledge of what we need is in our brains — we'd lose more time transmitting this knowledge to an AI with a very likely chance of missing important information.

Maybe AI will also reach this dimension and do a good job at it. It can eventually build access to all the information it needs and do the planning for us. But I think we're safe to pursue this orchestration path for some years. Once it reaches this dimension, we will already be talking about an almost complete replacement of humans — AI managing itself with practically no safeguards. That's hard to grasp, even though some people are already trying it.

What I've been doing: sketching before I prompt. Drawing the system, defining the boundaries, deciding what matters. The clearer the blueprint, the better AI executes — and the easier it is to spot when it goes off track.

2. Knowledge Management

If AI has enough knowledge, it will be able to do proper planning and a much better job. We're already seeing this in practice: teams writing instruction files that specify what they expect from AI, companies building internal wikis and onboarding guides that double as context for AI assistants. When we implement AI in companies, we spend a lot of time building access to knowledge — or the knowledge itself — to be used by AI.

Knowledge Management is already a very important area in large companies and associations. It is usually perceived as a bureaucratic process that slows things down, but effectively, it helps create structures that enable workers to go further than what has been done up to that point. In a similar way, AI will be favored by organized knowledge: it needs to know its context so it can go one step further. If we don't work on this, AI will only be good for projects built from scratch — and every project eventually inherits a past.

Document decisions, maintain context, structure what your team knows. The way I see it, every piece of well-organized knowledge is a better prompt waiting to happen.

This whole transition coincides with a slight career sidestep of mine — I am focusing more on planning and managing teams. It seemed like the right time, since we are becoming orchestrators of work: I have enough technical knowledge to understand the output and structure it, and there are emerging ideas about how we can manage knowledge to help us scale.

I was not an early adopter, but two months ago I decided to change the way I worked. Instead of opening my code editor, I started speaking with a Code Assistant, guiding it to the intended outcome. A few months before that, this wouldn't work — it easily got out of control, it even deleted two of my databases (many people relate!). Today the time saved significantly outweighs the time wasted debugging.

Like many others I am trying to find meaning in the middle of this work revolution. It hasn't been even a decade since I started working and I already have to think about what comes next. Of course I can continue doing software development in hyperspeed mode, but during the past months it doesn't seem right — I would simply listen to my body and understand that I am limited and can't keep up.

I bet people coding in Assembly — a very low-level programming language, where you control every tiny detail — felt the same when languages that handled those details for them showed up. We need to learn to trust that things work even though we weren't the ones doing everything, and still assume responsibility for them.

The challenge is to build a system that allows us to trust AI. My proposal: give more emphasis to planning and knowledge. Spend more time researching, building reports, drawing, organizing what you know. We have an opportunity to become what Peter Drucker called "knowledge workers" — a concept later explored by Stephen Covey in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People — people whose primary value is what they know and how they organize it.
